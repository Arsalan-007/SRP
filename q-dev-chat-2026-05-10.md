## Key Design Decisions

Causal mask: token `i` can only attend to `0..i`, and the CLS token (last) attends to all — exactly like your gradient boosting analogy. This is enforced via `torch.triu(..., diagonal=1)` filled with `-inf`.

Diversity: two complementary measures are used together:
- Cosine similarity catches directional similarity between feature vectors
- Pearson correlation catches linear redundancy in what the MLPs learned

The diversity loss is minimized (lower = more diverse), so setting `diversity_weight > 0` actively pushes MLPs apart during training.

## The negative div values — what does that mean?

This is the key thing to notice. When `diversity_weight > 0`, the optimizer is actively minimizing the diversity loss, which means it's pushing the cosine similarity and correlation between MLPs toward zero and then below zero (anti-correlation). A negative value means the MLP output vectors are pointing in roughly opposite directions on average — they've been pushed past "different" into "opposite". That's why too high a weight hurts: the MLPs are spending capacity being anti-correlated rather than being useful.

The sweet spot here is `div_weight=0.05` — a small nudge toward diversity improved generalization (0.2855 → 0.2814). Beyond that, the diversity penalty dominates and the model overfits to being diverse rather than being accurate. This is exactly the tension your idea is built around — diversity should be a regularizer, not the primary objective.

---

## 1. Removing the CLS Token

Right now the CLS token is a learnable vector appended at the end of the K MLP tokens, and the final prediction comes from its output after attention. Without it, you need a different way to aggregate the K token outputs into a single prediction.

Your options:

- **Mean pooling** — average all K token outputs after attention, pass that to the head. Simplest, treats all MLPs equally.
- **Last token** — use the output of the last MLP token (token K) since under causal attention it has already attended to all previous tokens, similar to how GPT uses the last token. This is the most natural fit for your sequential/boosting analogy.
- **Weighted sum** — learn a small vector of K weights (softmaxed), take a weighted sum of the K outputs. Adds a tiny bit of parameters but lets the model decide which MLPs matter more.

The last token approach is the most theoretically motivated for your idea — MLP K has seen everything before it, so it's the most "informed" token, which mirrors gradient boosting where the last tree has the most context.

---

## 2. Weight Scheduling for the Diversity Loss

Right now `diversity_weight` is a fixed constant throughout training. The idea of scheduling it is to change how much diversity pressure is applied over time. Two natural strategies:

- **Warm-up (ramp up)** — start with `diversity_weight=0` and gradually increase it to a target value over the first N epochs. The idea is to let the MLPs first learn something useful about the data, then push them apart. If you apply diversity pressure too early, the MLPs haven't learned anything meaningful yet so there's nothing useful to diversify.

- **Annealing (ramp down)** — start with high diversity pressure and reduce it over time. Force diversity early when the MLPs are being initialized, then let the task loss take over for fine-tuning.

The warm-up strategy is more aligned with your idea conceptually — it mirrors how in boosting, each tree first fits the residual (task), and diversity is a consequence of the sequential structure, not forced from the start.

---

## What to actually implement

In `train.py`, instead of a fixed `diversity_weight` scalar, you'd pass a schedule — either a callable `diversity_weight(epoch)` or just compute it inline each epoch. Something like:

```
# warm-up over first 30 epochs
div_w = min(target_weight, target_weight * epoch / warmup_epochs)
```

Then the ablation in the notebook becomes a 2D grid: diversity weight target × schedule type, which gives you a much richer picture of what's actually driving the performance.
